A data center, redesigned for orbit

The main barrier is radiation. Our research is built to remove it in partner labs before the first flight; the flight validates what labs cannot reproduce.

Orbital sunrise from the ISS · NASA

The physics

FactorFigure and assumption
SunlightAbout 1,361 W/m² above the atmosphere, near-continuous in a dawn-dusk orbit
Solar arrayAbout 400 W/m² at roughly 30% cell efficiency
Heat rejectionHeat leaves only as radiation: a 300 K radiator rejects about 410 W/m² per face
Radiator sizeA 500 W payload needs roughly 1.2–1.5 m² of radiator
OrbitLow Earth orbit below 600 km; deorbit by natural decay at end of life

Radiation, in layers

Shielding. A trade study compares aluminium, spot shielding and layered materials to find the lightest option that meets the dose target. Hardware. A supervisor circuit detects a latch-up, cuts power almost instantly and restarts the processor. Software. Error-correcting memory and checkpointing. Network. The mesh moves interrupted work to a healthy node, so a radiation event costs no work. Orbit. The mesh schedules sensitive work around the polar regions and the South Atlantic Anomaly.

Heat and energy

Low-power chips cut heat at the source, heat pipes carry it to deployable radiators, and a dawn-dusk orbit keeps the load steady. The mesh throttles work and routes it to nodes with thermal headroom.

Heleo-1, the reference satellite

Heleo-1 is the reference satellite for the Heleos Mesh in orbit. It is a concept; none has flown.

SatellitePlan
Heleo-1First orbital demonstration, Year 3. About 60 kg on a commercial bus, rideshare launch, dawn-dusk orbit
Heleo-2 to Heleo-4Year 4. Inclined 53°, polar and near-equatorial orbits with inter-satellite links, so work can route across orbit planes

Launch and servicing

Designed for rideshare missions with a launch partner. Satellites are replaced, not repaired; software updates go over the air; and the mesh routes around any failed node. Our economic model states the launch price at which orbital compute matches ground data centers as an explicit assumption: today orbital compute costs more per GPU-hour, and the case depends on launch costs falling.
